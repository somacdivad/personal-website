Today I read the essay _On Sadness_.
It begins with a shocking assertion:

> I am among those who are most free from this emotion: I neither like it nor think well of it, even though the world, by common consent, has decided to honour it with special favour.

What does Montaigne mean by this?
By being "free from this emotion," is he saying he does not experience it?
That seems unlikely.

Indeed, the rest of the essay suggests that what Montaigne is "free" of is an attachment to sadness as a virtuous feeling.

> The force of extreme sadness inevitably stuns the whole of our soul, impeding her freedom of action.
> It happens to us when we are suddenly struck with alarm by some really bad news: we are enraptured, seized, paralyzed in all of our movements in such a way that, afterwards, when the soul lets herself go with tears and lamentations, she seems to have struggled loose, disentangled herself and become free to range about as she wishes.

Could that have been written by a man who has not experienced sadness?
It should be noted that Montaigne's project of writing the essays was colored by the death of his father and a beloved friend.

Montaigne's dislike of sadness appears to be rooted in its ability to incapacitate us.
So powerful is its effect that it can cause that "temporary impotence which sometimes comes so unseasonably upon men when making love."
Even joy, the opposite of sadness, can overwhelm us.

> For pleasures to be tasted and then digested they must remain moderate.

So the goal is not to become unfeeling, but to maintain control over our emotions.
To "taste" an emotion is to experience it.
To "digest" it is to process it, to integrate it into our being, to productively benefit from the experience.

> Violent emotions like these have little hold on me.
> By nature my sense of feeling has a hard skin, which I daily toughen and thicken by arguments.

One has to wonder how aspirational that first sentence is.

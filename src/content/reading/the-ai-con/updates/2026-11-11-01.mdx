Chapter 4 is titled _If It Quacks Like a Doc: AI Hype and Social Services_.
It covers how AI is being used, misused, abused, and hyped in criminal justice, health care, social services, and education.

There's a lot of overlap here with Hannah Fry's excellent book _Hello World_, including how judges are using anti-recidivism tools to decide who deserves parole, how lawyers have used ChatGPT to write court briefs and argue cases (often riddled with erroneous and invented citations), how hospitals and clinics use AI tools to decide who gets care, for how long, and at what price, and how schools are encouraging teachers to use AI to build lesson plans, grade homework, and assess students.

Tech companies use benchmarks to "prove" that their models can perform these tasks.
But what they fail to understand, or perhaps simply ignore, is that these benchmarks, in literally all cases, judge only how well a model can generate text that meets the standards of the benchmark.
Just because you can pick the right multiple-choice question on a medical exam does not mean you will listen intently to a patient, diagnose their ailment carefully, and prescribe treatments that take their true needs into account.

Of course there are a plethora of human doctors that also do not meet this standard, but there are more that do than there would be if care were entirely automated. And there are networks of humans working now that help hold each other accountable.
There can be no accountability if the entire chain is automated.

The authors hit the nail on the head in the closing paragraphs of the chapter:

> Just because you've identified a social problem doesn't mean LLMs or any other kind of so-called AI are a solution.
> When someone says so, the problem is usually better understood by widening the lens, looking at it in its broader context.
> As Chankar Narayan, the Tech and Liberty Project director for ACLU of Washington, asked regarding biased recidivism prediction systems:
> **Why are we asking who is most likely to reoffend rather than what these people need to give them the best chance of not reoffending?**

Too many people in tech care only about "solving" problems for the sake of "solving" problems.
Too often they conflate "solve" with "pass a standardized test."
They almost never ask the right questions.

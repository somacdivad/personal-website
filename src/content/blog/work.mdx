A man looks out from a city apartment window as the sun rises behind an old red masonry art deco building. A tower thrusts its triangulated steel skeleton high above, separating a lone morning-blushed cloud from open sky.

Who in these pictures is getting more work done?

It's a great failure of our age that we consider hustle a virtue, but dismiss as lazy a quiet hour by a window spent contemplating what's keeping you from where you need to be. Doing the hard work, it turns out, can sometimes look like taking it easy.

---

**Work** is an ancient word. It comes from the [Proto-Indo-European](https://www.deadlanguagesociety.com/p/what-came-before-english) root *wérǵ*, meaning to "do" or "make." But it's not all about toil. *Wérǵ* can be pleasurable, being also a root of the word *orgia*, which is a Greek word for a good time.

*Wérǵ* is also the root of the Greek word *ergon* (ἔργον), which Aristotle uses to describe the "work" or "function" of the human being in his popular thriller *Nichomachean Ethics*:[^aristotle-function]

Our life's work, if we believe Aristotle, is to exercise reason (the soul's faculties) and exercise it well (in conformity with virtue). To use our mind to wisely direct our actions. Noble words that are easy to agree with.

But how do we build a wise society? Fortunately, Aristotle offers us a recipe. In his ideal society:[^aristotle-leisure]

<Translation
  original="οὔτε βάναυσον βίον οὔτ᾽ ἀγοραῖον δεῖ ζῆν τοὺς πολίτας (ἀγεννὴς γὰρ ὁ τοιοῦτος βίος καὶ πρὸς ἀρετὴν ὑπεναντίος), οὐδὲ δὴ γεωργοὺς εἶναι τοὺς μέλλοντας ἔσεσθαι (δεῖ γὰρ σχολῆς καὶ πρὸς τὴν γένεσιν τῆς ἀρετῆς καὶ πρὸς τὰς πράξεις τὰς πολιτικάς)."
  translation="the citizens must not lead the life of mechanics or tradesmen, for such a life is ignoble, and inimical to virtue. Neither must they be husbandmen, since leisure is necessary both for the development of virtue and the performance of political duties."
  author="Aristotle"
  work="Politics"
  reference="Book 7, chapter 9 (1328b39–1329a2)"
  sourceUrl="https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.7.seven.html"
  showCitation={false}
/>

According to Aristotle, the best way to grow a wise society is to have a class of laborers supporting a life of leisure for a governing class who get to spend their all their free time flourishing. Ignoble words that are hard to agree with.

His society has two classes, both *wérǵ*ing. But who benefits?

Aristotle shows us that too much contemplation can cause confusion, but it's nothing a bit of gardening or pottery or woodworking or weaving or milling or hammering or digging can't cure.
